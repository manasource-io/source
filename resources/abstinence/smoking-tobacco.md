Tobacco smoking is one of the leading preventable causes of premature death worldwide. The Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 attributes more than 175 million deaths and nearly 4.30 billion years of life lost to smoking between 1990 and 2021, and more than one in ten deaths globally in 2021 alone. Smokers who do not stop lose an average of about 10 years of life expectancy compared with never-smokers, and they begin to suffer diseases of old age around 10 years earlier. Most smoking-related deaths arise from cancers (mainly lung cancer), respiratory disease (mainly chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), and cardiovascular disease (mainly coronary heart disease); smoking is also an important risk factor for stroke, Alzheimer's and vascular dementia, osteoporosis, blindness, deafness, peripheral vascular disease, reduced fertility, and harm in pregnancy. Stopping at any age is beneficial compared with continuing, but the shape of that benefit differs by disease: for some the risk is reversed, and for others it is approximately frozen at the point when smoking stopped.

## Life Expectancy {#life-expectancy}

A pooled analysis of four national cohorts with death-registry linkage in the United States, United Kingdom, Norway, and Canada followed 1.48 million adults for 15 years, during which 122,697 deaths occurred. Survival between ages 40 and 79 was 12 years shorter in women and 13 years shorter in men who smoked than in never-smokers, which the authors describe as about 24 to 26 years of life lost among the smokers who died from smoking combined with zero loss among those who did not. Compared with continued smoking, cessation of fewer than 3 years potentially averted 5 years of life lost, and cessation of 10 or more years averted about 10 years of life lost, yielding survival similar to that of never-smokers. Cessation at every age was associated with longer survival, particularly cessation before age 40.

A review of smoking's health impact reports the same pattern from earlier cohorts: smokers who stop before their mid-30s have approximately the same life expectancy as never-smokers, and after about age 35, stopping recovers 2–3 months of healthy life expectancy for every year of smoking avoided. These are observational associations, not randomized comparisons, and none of the sources cited here quantifies life-years recovered specifically for quitting by age 35.

At population scale, the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 forecast that eliminating smoking from 2023 onwards would avert 2.04 billion cumulative years of life lost by 2050 compared with a reference scenario in which recent prevalence trends continue. That is a modelled scenario for whole populations, not a measured gain for an individual who quits.

## All-Cause Mortality {#all-cause-mortality}

In the pooled four-country analysis, current smokers had all-cause mortality hazard ratios of 2.8 in women and 2.7 in men versus never-smokers after adjustment for age, education, alcohol use, and obesity, while former smokers had a hazard ratio of 1.3 in both sexes. A US prospective cohort of 551,388 adults from the National Health Interview Survey, with 74,870 deaths through 2019, found an all-cause mortality rate ratio of 2.80 (95% CI, 2.73–2.88) for current versus never smoking and 1.37 (95% CI, 1.34–1.40) for former versus never smoking. Rate ratios were similar by sex but varied by race and ethnicity: 2.01 (95% CI, 1.84–2.18) among Hispanic adults, 2.19 (95% CI, 2.06–2.33) among non-Hispanic Black adults, 3.00 (95% CI, 2.91–3.10) among non-Hispanic White adults, and 2.16 (95% CI, 1.88–2.47) among adults of other non-Hispanic race and ethnicity. Current smoking therefore carried at least twice the all-cause mortality rate of never smoking in every group.

The same cohort quantifies what quitting is associated with. Compared with never-smokers, the all-cause mortality rate ratio was 1.03 (95% CI, 0.99–1.07) among those who quit before age 35, 1.21 (95% CI, 1.17–1.26) at ages 35 to 44, 1.47 (95% CI, 1.42–1.53) at ages 45 to 54, and 1.74 (95% CI, 1.68–1.80) at ages 55 to 64. By time since quitting, it fell from 1.72 (95% CI, 1.65–1.79) at 5 to 14 years to 1.04 (95% CI, 1.00–1.08) at 35 years or more. Quitting before age 45 was associated with removing approximately 90% of the excess mortality associated with continued smoking, and quitting at ages 45 to 64 with removing approximately 66%, consistently across racial and ethnic groups. Both studies are observational, and the US authors state their attributable-fraction estimates only on the assumption that the associations are causal.

## Cardiovascular Health {#cardiovascular-health}

Cardiovascular disease, mainly coronary heart disease, is one of the three categories that account for most smoking-related deaths. The clearest recovery figure in these sources concerns heart attack: the excess risk of heart attack caused by smoking reduces by 50% within 12 months of stopping smoking. For coronary heart disease and stroke, the same review describes the risk as preventable if cessation occurs in early adulthood and at least partially reversible thereafter. It also notes that the association between daily cigarette consumption and cardiovascular disease is non-linear, so low consumption carries a higher risk than a straight-line extrapolation would predict.

In the US cohort, the cardiovascular mortality rate ratio was 2.34 (95% CI, 2.24–2.45) for current versus never smoking and 1.23 (95% CI, 1.18–1.28) for former versus never smoking. The pooled four-country analysis found lower excess mortality from vascular disease after cessation, with beneficial associations evident as early as 3 years after quitting. None of these sources documents a minutes-after-the-last-cigarette timeline, and none shows overall cardiovascular risk returning to never-smoker levels after 10 to 15 years.

## Cancer Risk {#cancer-risk}

Cancers, mainly lung cancer, are the largest single category of smoking-related death, and what cessation does to that risk depends on how the risk is measured. The review is explicit that stopping smoking "freezes" the risk of smoking-related cancers at the level reached when smoking stopped and does not decrease it in absolute terms. The benefit is that the further increase continued smoking would bring is prevented, and that cancers of the lung and upper airways are preventable if cessation occurs in early adulthood.

Measured against people who keep smoking, former smokers nonetheless carry far less excess cancer mortality. In the US cohort, the cancer mortality rate ratio was 3.38 (95% CI, 3.22–3.55) for current versus never smoking and 1.52 (95% CI, 1.46–1.59) for former versus never smoking, and the roughly 80% reduction in excess mortality seen for all causes was generally consistent for cancer deaths within each racial, ethnic, and sex group. The pooled four-country analysis likewise found lower excess mortality from neoplastic disease after quitting at any age, with the largest benefit among those who quit young. Neither study measures lung cancer incidence specifically, and none of these sources supports the claim that lung cancer risk keeps falling in absolute terms after cessation.

**On cessation:** Unaided quit attempts succeed long-term only about 5% of the time , not a failure of willpower, but a consequence of nicotine's powerful effects on dopaminergic reward circuits. Effective cessation support dramatically improves odds: **varenicline (Champix/Chantix)** combined with behavioral support achieves abstinence rates exceeding 40% at 52 weeks. Nicotine replacement therapy (NRT , patches, gum, lozenges) roughly doubles unaided quit success. Brief physician advice alone increases cessation rates by 1–3 percentage points , enough to have meaningful population-level impact. Combination NRT (a long-acting patch plus a short-acting form for cravings) outperforms single NRT. The evidence is clear: treating nicotine addiction as a medical condition and seeking evidence-based cessation support is the most reliable path to quitting.
