Aerobic exercise is the endurance type: any rhythmic, sustained activity that uses large muscle groups and relies on the body's oxygen-dependent energy systems. Jogging, cycling, swimming, brisk walking, rowing, and dancing are individual activities within this type rather than separate evidence resources. Each section below restates one of this resource's three curated claims and names the resource-local reference that claim cites; nothing about this type is asserted here beyond them.

## All-Cause Mortality {#all-cause-mortality}

Higher aerobic activity and fitness track lower all-cause mortality. The 2024 overview of meta-analyses of cardiorespiratory fitness, one of the two references this claim cites, synthesized 26 systematic reviews covering 20.9 million observations across 199 unique cohort studies and reported that **high cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) versus low CRF was associated with a 53% lower risk of all-cause mortality** (HR = 0.47), with each 1-MET increase in fitness corresponding to an **11–17% lower all-cause mortality risk**. The claim's other reference, a 2019 review of physical activity and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, reports the same direction for activity itself.

Both are observational syntheses of cohort data rather than randomized intervention effects. Neither is cited here for how much fitness a given person gains from training, or how quickly.

## Cardiovascular Health {#cardiovascular-health}

Aerobic activity shows a dose-dependent lower risk of cardiovascular disease and its mortality. The single reference cited for this claim is the 2019 review of physical activity, all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, and cardiovascular disease, and like the mortality claim it is observational. This resource cites no source for the cardiac, vascular, arterial, or blood-pressure mechanisms behind that association, or for which starting point gains most from it.

## Mental Health {#mental-health}

Aerobic exercise reduces anxiety and depressive symptoms and improves mood. The claim's one reference is the 2006 review *Exercise for mental health*, which reports that activities including jogging, swimming, cycling, walking, and dancing reduce anxiety and depression, with 30 minutes of moderate-intensity activity three days per week producing substantial benefit. That figure is what this review observed, not a corpus recommendation: no reference cited here establishes a weekly activity guideline for this type.
